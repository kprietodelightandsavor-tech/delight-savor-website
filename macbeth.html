Not another dusty Shakespeare unit

We hand students the book, not a summary of it

Most Macbeth study guides are comprehension questions, vocabulary lists, and a test. This one starts somewhere else: with the student telling back, in their own words, what they met in the reading. Charlotte Mason called narration “the art of knowing,” and it is the backbone of every week.

I taught AP Literature and AP Language in public school for nine years before I homeschooled, and I hold a master’s in Literary Studies. This study keeps the rigor of that classroom and changes what the rigor is for. The analysis is real. It simply grows out of what the student noticed, not out of what I told them to find.

When I returned to teaching Macbeth, I knew I didn’t want it to feel like just another dusty Shakespeare unit. I wanted it to breathe.

How the term moves

A Read week, then a Deep-Dive week

We go through the play act by act, alternating two kinds of weeks, so no one is asked to analyze a scene they have not yet simply read and told back.

Read week

Read the act aloud, together. Then narrate: oral at first, written as the term goes on. Some weeks the narration is a tableau, with students freezing a moment from the play into a living picture.

Deep-Dive week

Each one opens the same way:

  • The Habit of Attention
  • A Find It · Follow It · Frame It passage
  • A short writing lesson

The arts sit beside the text. We listen to Mussorgsky’s Night on Bald Mountain while reading Act I and look closely at Henry Fuseli’s Macbeth painting, so students meet Shakespeare as part of a larger cultural conversation and not as a play in isolation.

From narration to argument

Four moves that turn reading into writing

The essay never appears out of nowhere. A student who narrates faithfully for ten weeks has been gathering the evidence for a thesis without knowing it. Read the full approach.

  • Find ItNotice what is actually on the page.
  • Follow ItTrace it through the play, with the text as evidence.
  • Frame ItSay what it means, and why it matters.
  • Keep ItWrite it down: a narration, a commonplace entry, an essay.

Where the term gathers

A debate, an essay, and a square for the quilt

  1. A debate, where students argue the play out loud before they argue it on paper.
  2. A Follow-the-Thread essay. Each student follows one thread through the play and argues what it means.
  3. A square for the class Quilt, where every student answers the play back in their own hand. See the Conversation Quilt.

Questions parents ask

Before you begin

What grades is it for?

Grades 9–12, though honestly the better question is: is your student ready to sit with a hard book and have a real conversation about it? Grade is a guideline. The student is the guide.

Does my student need to have read Shakespeare before?

No. Narration doesn’t require prior knowledge, only honest engagement with the text. A student who has never read Shakespeare can absolutely begin with Macbeth.

Do I need to buy the play separately?

Yes. You’ll need a copy of Macbeth. Any standard edition works, and the play is in the public domain, so it is easy to find online or at a used bookstore.

My student struggles with writing. Will this work?

Yes, and this approach may be exactly what a struggling writer needs. A student who “can’t write” usually doesn’t yet know what they think. Narration addresses that at the root, and the move from narration to essay is gradual by design. More questions answered here.
