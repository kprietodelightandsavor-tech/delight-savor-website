How I Plan Our Homeschool

Structured Enough to Hold, Flexible Enough to Bend

Kim Prieto · March 21, 2026

Let me tell you what “gentle rhythms” actually looks like in my house.

It’s 9:15 and the kids are supposed to be in Bible time. Instead, one of them is upset about something that happened at co-op yesterday, another is hungry because they chose cereal for breakfast and burned through it in forty minutes, and I’m standing in the kitchen wondering if this counts as a bad day or just a Tuesday.

This is my fifth year homeschooling. And the thing I’ve learned — slowly, with a lot of resistance — is that the goal was never a perfect day. The goal is a rhythm sturdy enough that when life interrupts it, you can find your way back.

That’s the whole thing. That’s what I’m trying to build.

Structure is not a schedule. It’s a sequence.

I used to confuse these two things, and it made me miserable. A schedule says: Bible at 9:00, language arts at 10:15, history at 11:30. A sequence says: we always do Bible before academics, we always do movement before we ask for deep focus, we always come back together after lunch.

The sequence holds even when the clock doesn’t.

On a hard morning, if we don’t get to Bible until 10:00, that’s okay — the rhythm still holds. The kids still got their independent launch time first. We still gathered before the academic work. We still moved before we asked their brains to focus. Nothing was lost except the numbers on a clock, and those were never the point.

What I’ve built our days around is anchors, not appointments. Rise and Shine is the first anchor — the kids work independently on math, language arts, and piano while I get myself ready and the animals fed. That anchor doesn’t move. It’s what starts our engine.

Then Bible, breakfast, and Beauty Loop. That’s our gathering point, our family heartbeat. Thirty minutes. Non-negotiable. After that, movement and a break. Then into the academic work — math first, while their brains are freshest, with language arts following. The history, science, and geography loop moves to the afternoon, when we need something a little more relational and less computationally demanding. I found this out by watching my kids, not by following a plan — they simply focus better on numbers before lunch. So we adjusted.

The sequence is the structure. The times are just estimates.

Flexibility isn’t the absence of a plan. It’s what makes the plan survivable.

Here’s what I do when we miss something: I don’t make it up. I don’t double up tomorrow. I retroactively let it go and trust that we’ll get back to it in its natural rotation.

Missed history today? It’ll come around again. Didn’t get to the science reading? It’s still there waiting when we loop back. I stopped trying to keep up with every curriculum on its exact schedule the year I realized that the anxiety of being “behind” was costing us more than whatever lesson we’d missed.

What I do keep track of is where we are in each subject, not where I planned to be. Those are different numbers, and only one of them is useful.

This doesn’t mean we school loosely. We do serious, rigorous work. My kids narrate, they write, they read hard books and discuss harder ideas. But the rigor lives in the quality of the work, not in the number of boxes checked before noon.

The tools I use

I need something to hold all of this — the sequence, the subjects, the rotation, the weeks — so my brain doesn’t have to.

For digital planning, I built and an app designed around exactly this philosophy - I named it Tend. It has a term planner, a daily view by student, a habit tracker, an outdoor hours log, and a narration coach, all in one place. It’s built for the Charlotte Mason mom who needs structure but lives in the real world. You can find it at delightandsavor.com.

For those who prefer paper — and I am still a paper person at heart — I have a traditional planner in PDF form that you can print out or upload into GoodNotes or any annotation app. It’s fully customizable in Canva, so you can adjust it to fit your subjects, your number of children, your family’s specific rhythm. Some weeks I print a fresh week. Some weeks I annotate on my iPad. I use whatever version I’ll actually look at.

Both tools are designed around the same idea: your plan should bend with your life, not fight it.

One more thing worth saying: this approach works with whatever curriculum you use. I pull from four different curricula depending on the subject and the child — and none of them know that. The planning layer sits on top of everything, organizing the execution in a way that makes sense for our family. The curriculum provides the content; the rhythm provides the container. I will write more about the specific curricula I use and why in a future post, but I want you to know now that you do not need to overhaul what you are using to make this work.

What I want you to take away

If your homeschool days feel like they’re constantly falling apart, I want to gently suggest that the problem might not be your execution. It might be that the structure you’re holding yourself to wasn’t built for the life you’re actually living.

A sequence you can trust is worth more than a perfect schedule you can’t keep. A plan that bends is worth more than a plan that breaks.

This isn’t about lowering the bar. It’s about building the right bar — one your whole family can actually reach, on the beautiful days and on the Tuesdays.
